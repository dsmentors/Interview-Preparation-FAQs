KPI fundamentals

Q1What is the difference between a metric and a KPI?

A metric is any number you can measure (page views, tickets closed, revenue). A KPI (Key Performance Indicator) is a metric that is directly tied to a strategic business goal and is tracked to decide whether you are on target.

All KPIs are metrics, but most metrics are not KPIs. For example, “number of website visits” is a metric, while “conversion rate of visitors to paying customers”, tracked against a target to grow revenue, is a KPI.

Q2What makes a good KPI? What is a SMART KPI?

A good KPI is linked to a goal, measurable, understood by everyone, and actionable, meaning that the team can influence it. SMART is a useful checklist:

  • Specific: clearly defined.
  • Measurable: can be quantified from reliable data.
  • Achievable: realistic.
  • Relevant: connected to a business objective.
  • Time-bound: has a deadline or period.

Example: “Reduce customer churn from 5% to 4% per month within 6 months.”

Q3What is the difference between leading and lagging indicators?
Leading indicator Lagging indicator
What it shows Activity that predicts future results The result that has already happened
Easy to influence? Yes, you can act on it now Harder, because it reflects past actions
Examples Sales pipeline, trial sign-ups, website traffic, training hours Revenue, profit, churn, customer satisfaction

A balanced KPI set uses both: lagging indicators confirm the outcome, and leading indicators give early warning and something to act on.

Q4What is the difference between KPIs and OKRs?
  • KPIs track the ongoing health of the business or a process (“are we performing as expected?”), and they usually stay the same over time.
  • OKRs (Objectives and Key Results) are a goal-setting framework for a period such as a quarter: an inspiring objective with 2 to 5 measurable key results that stretch the team.

KPIs can be used as key results, and the two work well together: KPIs show where you stand, and OKRs set what you want to improve.

Q5What are vanity metrics and actionable metrics?
  • Vanity metrics look impressive but do not guide decisions or reflect real business value: total app downloads, total registered users, page views or social media followers.
  • Actionable metrics link to behavior and outcomes, so they tell you what to do next: conversion rate, activation rate, retention, revenue per customer.

A quick test: if the metric changes, do you know what action to take?

Q6What is a North Star metric?

The North Star metric is the single metric that best captures the core value your product delivers to customers, and that predicts long-term business success. It aligns the whole company, and other metrics (inputs) feed into it.

Example: for a messaging app it could be “weekly active conversations”, and for a streaming service “hours of content watched per subscriber”. It should reflect customer value, not just revenue.

Q7How do you choose the right KPIs for a business goal?
  1. Start from the business goal (for example, grow revenue from existing customers).
  2. Break it down into drivers (retention, upsell, purchase frequency).
  3. Choose a few KPIs per goal, a mix of leading and lagging ones. Too many KPIs create noise.
  4. Confirm the data is available and reliable, and agree on the exact definition of each one.
  5. Set targets, owners and a review frequency.
  6. Review regularly and drop KPIs that no longer drive decisions.
Q8What is Goodhart’s law, and how does it apply to KPIs?

“When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.” People start optimizing the number instead of the real goal. For example, if support agents are rewarded only for short call time, they may rush customers off the phone and lower satisfaction.

Reduce the risk with balanced KPIs (for example, call time together with customer satisfaction and first-contact resolution) and by adding guardrail metrics that must not get worse.

Sales, marketing and customer metrics

Q9What is Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC), and how is it calculated?

CAC is the average cost of winning one new customer.

CAC = total sales and marketing cost in a period / number of new customers acquired in that period

Example: spending 50,000 on sales and marketing and acquiring 250 customers gives a CAC of 200. It is often tracked by channel to see which channels are most efficient.

Q10What is Customer Lifetime Value (CLV or LTV), and what is a good LTV:CAC ratio?

LTV estimates the total profit (or revenue) a customer brings over their relationship with you. A simple formula:

LTV = average revenue per customer per period x gross margin % / churn rate per period

The LTV:CAC ratio compares the value of a customer with the cost of acquiring them. A common rule of thumb is 3:1 or higher. A ratio near 1:1 means you barely recover the acquisition cost, and a very high ratio may mean you are under-investing in growth.

Q11What is the CAC payback period?

It is how many months it takes to earn back the cost of acquiring a customer:

CAC payback (months) = CAC / (monthly revenue per customer x gross margin %)

A shorter payback means cash comes back faster. Many subscription businesses aim for well under 12 months.

Q12What is the conversion rate, and what is a conversion funnel?
Conversion rate = number of conversions / number of visitors (or leads) x 100

A funnel shows the steps users take toward a goal and how many drop off at each step, for example: visit → view product → add to cart → checkout → purchase. Analyzing the drop between steps shows where to improve.

SELECT
  COUNT(DISTINCT visitor_id) AS visitors,
  COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN step = 'add_to_cart' THEN visitor_id END) AS added_to_cart,
  COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN step = 'purchase' THEN visitor_id END) AS purchased
FROM events
WHERE event_date >= '2024-01-01';
Q13What are churn rate and retention rate?
Customer churn rate = customers lost during the period / customers at the start of the period x 100
Retention rate = 100% - churn rate

Example: starting the month with 1,000 customers and losing 50 gives a churn rate of 5% and a retention rate of 95%.

Churn can also be measured in revenue (revenue churn). Because small monthly churn compounds over a year, even 5% a month is a serious problem.

Q14What are NPS, CSAT and CES?
Metric Question asked How it is calculated
NPS (Net Promoter Score) “How likely are you to recommend us?” (0 to 10) % promoters (9 to 10) minus % detractors (0 to 6); passives (7 to 8) are ignored. Range −100 to +100
CSAT (Customer Satisfaction) “How satisfied were you with this interaction?” (1 to 5) % of satisfied answers (usually 4 and 5)
CES (Customer Effort Score) “How easy was it to get your issue resolved?” Average score on an effort scale

NPS measures loyalty, CSAT measures satisfaction with a specific experience, and CES measures ease.

Q15What are ARPU and AOV?
  • ARPU (Average Revenue Per User) = total revenue / number of users in the period. It shows the revenue earned per user.
  • AOV (Average Order Value) = total revenue / number of orders. It shows how much customers spend per purchase, and is a lever in e-commerce (for example through bundles or free-shipping thresholds).
Q16What are ROI, ROAS and the common digital marketing metrics?
Metric Formula Meaning
ROI (net profit from an investment / cost of the investment) x 100 Overall return
ROAS revenue from ads / ad spend Revenue earned per unit spent on ads (before costs of goods)
CTR clicks / impressions x 100 How engaging the ad is
CPC ad cost / clicks Cost per click
CPM ad cost / impressions x 1,000 Cost per 1,000 impressions
CPA ad cost / conversions Cost per acquisition or conversion

ROAS ignores profit margins, so a ROAS of 3 is not always profitable. ROI accounts for costs.

Q17What is cohort analysis, and why is it useful?

A cohort is a group of users who share a starting point, for example everyone who signed up in January. Cohort analysis follows each group over time (week 1, week 2, ...) to see how metrics such as retention or revenue change as the customers age.

It reveals whether newer cohorts behave better or worse than older ones, which a simple overall average hides, and it shows the real effect of product or marketing changes.

Q18What is RFM analysis?

RFM segments customers by three behaviors: Recency (how recently they purchased), Frequency (how often they purchase) and Monetary value (how much they spend). Each customer gets a score for each, which identifies groups such as champions, loyal customers, at-risk customers and lost customers, so marketing can target each group differently.

Product and SaaS metrics

Q19What are DAU, MAU and the stickiness ratio?
  • DAU: daily active users. MAU: monthly active users.
  • Stickiness = DAU / MAU. It shows what share of monthly users come back on a typical day. A higher ratio means the product is used more habitually.

You must define what “active” means (for example performing a key action), otherwise the number is meaningless.

SELECT DATE_TRUNC('month', event_date) AS month,
       COUNT(DISTINCT user_id) AS mau
FROM events
GROUP BY 1
ORDER BY 1;
Q20What are MRR and ARR?
  • MRR (Monthly Recurring Revenue): the predictable subscription revenue per month.
  • ARR (Annual Recurring Revenue) = MRR x 12. It is common for annual contracts.

MRR is usually broken into new, expansion, contraction and churned MRR, which shows what is driving growth.

Q21What are Net Revenue Retention (NRR) and Gross Revenue Retention (GRR)?
NRR = (starting MRR + expansion - contraction - churn) / starting MRR x 100
GRR = (starting MRR - contraction - churn) / starting MRR x 100
  • NRR includes upgrades and upsells, so it can be above 100% when existing customers grow faster than the revenue lost.
  • GRR ignores expansion, so it cannot exceed 100% and shows how well you keep existing revenue.
Q22What are the activation rate and time to value?
  • Activation rate: the percentage of new users who reach the “aha moment”, a key action that shows they experienced the product’s value (for example creating their first project).
  • Time to value: how long it takes a new user to reach that moment.

Better activation and shorter time to value usually lead to higher retention.

Q23How do you analyze retention curves?

A retention curve plots the percentage of a cohort still active after day 1, day 7, day 30 and so on. A healthy product's curve flattens at some level, meaning a core group stays. If it keeps falling to zero, the product is not retaining users. Comparing curves between cohorts, platforms or features shows what improves retention.

Q24What is feature adoption, and how do you measure it?

Feature adoption shows how many users use a feature:

Adoption rate = users who used the feature / total active users x 100

Also look at depth (how often they use it) and whether users of the feature retain better than others. Low adoption can mean a discovery problem, a usability problem or a feature that nobody needs.

Q25What are burn rate and runway?
  • Burn rate: the net cash a company spends per month (cash out minus cash in).
  • Runway = current cash / monthly net burn rate. It shows how many months the company can operate before running out of money.

They are the key health metrics for startups: a runway of 12 to 18 months or more is generally considered comfortable.

Finance and operations metrics

Q26What are gross margin, net profit margin and EBITDA?
Gross margin %      = (revenue - cost of goods sold) / revenue x 100
Net profit margin % = net income / revenue x 100
  • EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. It approximates operating performance without financing and accounting effects, which makes companies easier to compare.
Q27How do you calculate the break-even point?

The break-even point is the sales volume at which total revenue equals total costs, so profit is zero:

Break-even units = fixed costs / (price per unit - variable cost per unit)

Example: fixed costs of 100,000, a price of 50 and a variable cost of 30 give a contribution of 20 per unit, so break-even is 100,000 / 20 = 5,000 units.

Q28What are ROA and ROE?
  • ROA (Return on Assets) = net income / total assets. It shows how efficiently assets generate profit.
  • ROE (Return on Equity) = net income / shareholders’ equity. It shows the return generated for shareholders.

ROE can be inflated by high debt, so read it together with debt metrics.

Q29What is working capital, and why does cash flow matter more than profit for survival?

Working capital = current assets − current liabilities. It shows the ability to pay short-term obligations. A company can be profitable on paper but run out of cash (for example when customers pay late while suppliers and salaries must be paid now), and running out of cash is what actually closes a business.

Q30What are common supply chain and operations KPIs?
  • Inventory turnover = cost of goods sold / average inventory. It shows how fast stock sells.
  • OTIF (On-Time In-Full): the share of orders delivered on time and complete.
  • Order cycle time and lead time.
  • Capacity utilization = actual output / maximum possible output.
  • Defect rate and first-pass yield (the share of units made right the first time).
  • Cost per unit.
Q31What are common customer support KPIs?
  • First Response Time (FRT) and average resolution time.
  • First Contact Resolution (FCR) = issues resolved at the first contact / total issues.
  • SLA adherence: the share of tickets handled within the agreed time.
  • Ticket backlog and ticket volume trends.
  • CSAT and CES after interactions.

Combine speed metrics with quality metrics, so agents are not pushed to close tickets quickly at the cost of satisfaction.

Q32What are common HR KPIs?
  • Attrition (turnover) rate = employees who left / average number of employees x 100.
  • Time to hire and cost per hire.
  • Offer acceptance rate.
  • Employee engagement or eNPS (employee Net Promoter Score).
  • Absenteeism rate.
  • Training hours and internal promotion rate.

Analysis, dashboards and pitfalls

Q33A key metric suddenly dropped by 15%. How do you investigate?
  1. Verify the data first: is it a tracking error, a delayed pipeline, a changed definition or a bug in the dashboard?
  2. Check the scale and timing: when exactly did it start, and is it still going on? Compare with the same period last week or year (seasonality).
  3. Break it down by segment: product, region, channel, device, customer type, new vs returning. Find where the drop is concentrated.
  4. Look at the funnel or the formula: metrics are ratios, so check whether the numerator fell or the denominator grew.
  5. List possible causes (internal changes such as releases, pricing, campaigns, outages; external changes such as competitors, holidays, news) and test them against the data.
  6. Recommend the action and the follow-up monitoring.
Q34What are guardrail metrics?

Guardrail metrics are measures that must not get worse while you optimize a main metric. For example, when you push for more sign-ups, the guardrails could be refund rate, customer complaints or page load time. They protect against “winning” the main metric while harming the business.

Q35What is the difference between correlation and causation, and how do you prove that a change caused a metric to move?

Two metrics moving together (correlation) does not mean that one caused the other, because a third factor may drive both. To establish causation, run a controlled experiment (A/B test), randomly assigning users to the change or no change. When experiments are not possible, use careful methods such as difference-in-differences or matched comparisons, and stay cautious about the conclusions.

Q36What is Simpson’s paradox?

It occurs when a trend that appears in several groups reverses or disappears when the groups are combined. For example, treatment A can have a higher success rate than B in every hospital, yet a lower rate overall, because A was used more on harder cases. It is a reminder to segment the data and think about group sizes before drawing conclusions from overall averages.

Q37Mean vs median: which one should you report for metrics such as order value or response time?

Use the median (and percentiles such as the 90th or 95th) when the data is skewed or has outliers, which is typical for order values, revenue per customer and response times. The mean can be pulled up by a few extreme values. For response times, percentiles (p90, p95) show the experience of the slowest users, which the average hides.

Q38Why should you use both absolute numbers and ratios?

Ratios (rates, percentages) allow fair comparison between groups of different sizes, but they can hide scale: a conversion rate that rises from 1% to 2% on 100 visitors is just 1 extra order. Absolute numbers show the real impact. Present both, and always give the denominator (the base) behind a percentage.

Q39What are the principles of a good KPI dashboard?
  • Start with the audience and the decisions they need to make.
  • Show only the few most important KPIs at the top, with a clear target or comparison (versus target, last period, last year).
  • Choose the right chart for the question: lines for trends, bars for comparisons, tables for exact values, and avoid pie charts with many slices.
  • Allow drill-down and filters for detail, without cluttering the main view.
  • Use consistent definitions, colors and formats, and color for meaning (for example red for off target).
  • State the data source and refresh time.
  • Tools: Power BI, Tableau, Looker and Excel.
Q40Why is a single, agreed metric definition important?

If sales, marketing and finance each calculate “active customer” or “revenue” differently, the numbers will not match and trust in the data is lost. Keep a data dictionary or metric catalog with the exact definition, formula, source, owner and filters of each KPI, and calculate it in one governed place (a semantic layer or a shared model) that every dashboard uses.

Case questions and scenarios

Q41Which KPIs would you track for an e-commerce website?
  • Traffic and acquisition: visitors by channel, CAC, ROAS.
  • Conversion: conversion rate, cart abandonment rate, checkout completion.
  • Order value: AOV and revenue per visitor.
  • Retention: repeat purchase rate, customer churn, LTV.
  • Operations: on-time delivery, return rate, stock-outs.
  • Satisfaction: NPS, CSAT, reviews.
  • Profitability: gross margin and contribution margin per order.
Q42How would you measure the success of a newly launched feature?
  1. Define the goal the feature is meant to serve (for example higher retention or faster task completion).
  2. Choose a primary metric (for example adoption, or the conversion rate of the related flow), supporting metrics and guardrail metrics.
  3. Set a baseline and a target before launch.
  4. Measure with an A/B test or a controlled rollout when possible.
  5. Look at adoption, depth of use and effect on retention or revenue, and segment by user type.
  6. Combine with qualitative feedback, and decide whether to scale, improve or remove the feature.
Q43How would you measure the health of a marketplace (buyers and sellers)?

Look at both sides and the match between them:

  • Supply: active sellers, listings, seller retention and quality.
  • Demand: active buyers, new vs repeat buyers, retention.
  • Liquidity: the share of listings that sell, time to first sale, search-to-purchase conversion.
  • Transactions: gross merchandise value (GMV), take rate and average order value.
  • Trust and quality: ratings, disputes, cancellations and fraud.
Q44Which KPIs would you use for a food delivery business?
  • Orders and growth: orders per day, new customers, repeat order rate, order frequency.
  • Experience: average delivery time, on-time delivery rate, order accuracy, ratings.
  • Unit economics: average order value, delivery cost per order, contribution margin per order, CAC and LTV.
  • Supply side: restaurant availability and acceptance rate, delivery partner utilization.
  • Cancellations and refunds.
Q45Which KPIs would you track for a SaaS company?
  • Revenue: MRR/ARR and its movements, ARPA.
  • Customers: new customers, logo churn, revenue churn, NRR and GRR.
  • Efficiency: CAC, LTV:CAC, CAC payback, the “magic number” or sales efficiency.
  • Product: activation, DAU/MAU, feature adoption, time to value.
  • Customer success: NPS, support tickets, health scores.
  • Cash: burn rate and runway.
Q46Revenue is growing but profit is falling. What could be the reasons?

Check the components of profit:

  • Margins: falling prices or discounts, higher cost of goods, a shift to lower-margin products.
  • Costs: rising marketing, staff, logistics or infrastructure costs, growing faster than revenue.
  • Customer quality: acquiring less profitable customers (higher CAC, lower LTV, more refunds or returns).
  • One-off items such as write-offs or exchange rate effects.

Break the change down by product, channel and customer segment to find where margin is lost.

Q47Two teams propose different success metrics for the same project. How do you decide?

Go back to the business goal and ask which metric reflects it best, and which one the team can actually influence. Compare the candidates on relevance, measurability, sensitivity to change, risk of being gamed (Goodhart’s law) and data quality. Often the answer is one primary metric plus supporting and guardrail metrics, agreed with the stakeholders and documented before the project starts.
