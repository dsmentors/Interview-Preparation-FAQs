Time series fundamentals

Q1What is a time series, and how is time series analysis different from regular regression?

A time series is a sequence of observations recorded in time order, usually at regular intervals (daily sales, hourly temperature, monthly revenue).

It differs from ordinary tabular modeling because:

  • Order matters: you cannot shuffle the rows.
  • Observations are dependent: today’s value is related to yesterday’s (autocorrelation), which breaks the independence assumption of many models.
  • Patterns evolve over time: trend, seasonality and changing variance.
  • Validation must respect time: you train on the past and test on the future.
Q2What are the components of a time series?
Component Meaning Example
Trend The long-term direction (up, down or flat) Growing sales over several years
Seasonality A regular pattern repeating over a fixed period Higher sales every December, or every weekend
Cyclic Rises and falls with no fixed period, usually tied to the economy or business cycles Economic expansions and recessions
Noise (irregular) Random variation left after removing the rest One-off events, measurement error
Q3What is the difference between additive and multiplicative time series?
  • Additive: y = Trend + Seasonality + Noise. The seasonal swings stay roughly the same size as the level changes.
  • Multiplicative: y = Trend × Seasonality × Noise. The seasonal swings grow with the level of the series.

If the seasonal amplitude increases as the series grows, use a multiplicative model, or take the log of the series, which turns a multiplicative pattern into an additive one.

Q4What is stationarity, and why does it matter?

A series is (weakly) stationary when its mean and variance are constant over time and its autocovariance depends only on the lag between points, not on the time itself. It has no trend and no changing seasonal pattern.

It matters because many classical models (AR, MA, ARIMA) assume stationarity: relationships learned from one period only apply to another if the statistical behavior is stable. If the series is not stationary, you transform it first (for example by differencing).

Q5How do you test whether a series is stationary?
  • Visual checks: plot the series and its rolling mean and variance.
  • ADF (Augmented Dickey-Fuller) test: the null hypothesis is that the series has a unit root (non-stationary). A p-value below 0.05 suggests the series is stationary.
  • KPSS test: the null hypothesis is that the series is stationary. A p-value below 0.05 suggests it is non-stationary.

Using both together gives a more reliable answer, because they have opposite null hypotheses.

from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import adfuller

result = adfuller(series.dropna())
print("ADF statistic:", result[0])
print("p-value:", result[1])        # p < 0.05 -> likely stationary
Q6How do you make a non-stationary series stationary?
  • Differencing: y_t - y_(t-1) removes a trend. Seasonal differencing (y_t - y_(t-s), where s is the season length) removes seasonality.
  • Log or Box-Cox transformation to stabilize a growing variance.
  • Detrending: subtract a fitted trend line or a moving average.
  • Decomposition: model the trend and seasonality separately and work on the residuals.

Do not over-difference: apply the smallest amount that makes the series stationary.

Q7What are white noise and a random walk?
  • White noise: a series of uncorrelated values with constant mean and variance. It has no pattern to forecast, and good model residuals should look like white noise.
  • Random walk: y_t = y_(t-1) + e_t. Each value is the previous value plus noise. It is non-stationary, and the best forecast is simply the last observed value. Differencing a random walk gives white noise.

Exploration and preprocessing

Q8How do you handle missing values and irregular timestamps in a time series?

First put the data on a regular frequency, then fill the gaps:

df = df.sort_index()
df = df.resample("D").sum()                     # use .mean() for rates, .sum() for counts
df["value"] = df["value"].interpolate(method="time")

Options for filling: forward fill, interpolation (linear or time-based), seasonal averages, or a model-based imputation. Choose carefully, because the wrong method can hide real patterns. Never use future values to fill past gaps when the data will be used to simulate real forecasting.

Q9What is resampling, and when would you use it?

Resampling changes the frequency of the series: downsampling (hourly to daily) aggregates the data, and upsampling (monthly to daily) creates new timestamps that you then fill. You use it to align several series to the same frequency, reduce noise, or forecast at the level the business needs. Pick the right aggregation: sum for totals (sales), mean for rates (temperature, price).

Q10What is decomposition, and how do you do it?

Decomposition splits a series into trend, seasonal and residual components, which helps you understand it, find anomalies in the residual, and build models. STL (Seasonal-Trend decomposition using LOESS) is flexible and robust to outliers.

from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import STL

stl = STL(series, period=12).fit()          # period = length of the season, e.g. 12 for monthly data
trend, seasonal, resid = stl.trend, stl.seasonal, stl.resid
stl.plot()
Q11What are ACF and PACF, and how do you read them?
  • ACF (autocorrelation function): the correlation between the series and its lagged copy, at each lag.
  • PACF (partial autocorrelation function): the correlation at a lag after removing the effect of the shorter lags.
Pattern ACF PACF
AR(p) Tails off gradually Cuts off after lag p
MA(q) Cuts off after lag q Tails off gradually

Spikes at regular intervals in the ACF (for example at lags 12, 24) indicate seasonality.

from statsmodels.graphics.tsaplots import plot_acf, plot_pacf

plot_acf(series, lags=40)
plot_pacf(series, lags=40)
Q12What is a moving average (smoothing), and what is exponential smoothing?
  • Moving average: the average of the last k observations. It smooths noise, but gives equal weight to all points in the window and lags behind turning points.
  • Exponential smoothing: a weighted average where recent observations get more weight and older ones decay exponentially, controlled by a smoothing factor α (between 0 and 1). A higher α reacts faster to changes.

The moving average smoother is different from the MA model in ARIMA, which models the dependence on past forecast errors.

Q13How do you create features from a time series for machine learning?
df["lag_1"] = df["sales"].shift(1)
df["lag_7"] = df["sales"].shift(7)
df["roll_mean_7"] = df["sales"].shift(1).rolling(7).mean()   # shift first, so the window only uses the past
df["dayofweek"] = df.index.dayofweek
df["month"] = df.index.month
df["is_holiday"] = df.index.isin(holiday_dates).astype(int)

Common features are lags, rolling and expanding statistics, calendar features (day of week, month, holidays) and external variables (price, promotions, weather). Always make sure every feature uses only information that would be available at forecast time.

Classical forecasting models

Q14What are baseline forecasts, and why start with them?
  • Naive: the forecast is the last observed value.
  • Seasonal naive: the forecast is the value from the same season last period (for example the same weekday last week).
  • Mean or drift methods.

Baselines are quick to build and hard to beat on some series. Any complex model must beat them to be worth using, so they give you a reference point for evaluation.

Q15What is exponential smoothing (ETS, Holt-Winters)?

A family of models that forecast with smoothed components:

Method Components Handles
Simple exponential smoothing Level No trend, no seasonality
Holt’s method Level + trend Trend
Holt-Winters Level + trend + seasonality Trend and seasonality (additive or multiplicative)

Each component has a smoothing parameter (α, β, γ) that is estimated from the data. ETS models are simple, fast and strong baselines for business forecasting.

Q16What are AR, MA, ARMA and ARIMA models?
  • AR(p), autoregressive: the value depends on its own previous p values.
  • MA(q), moving average: the value depends on the previous q forecast errors.
  • ARMA(p, q): combines both, for stationary series.
  • ARIMA(p, d, q): adds d rounds of differencing (the “I” for integrated), so it can handle a trend.
from statsmodels.tsa.arima.model import ARIMA

train, test = series[:-12], series[-12:]
model = ARIMA(train, order=(1, 1, 1)).fit()
forecast = model.forecast(steps=12)
Q17How do you choose the order (p, d, q) of an ARIMA model?
  1. d: difference until the series is stationary (ADF/KPSS tests), usually 0, 1 or 2.
  2. p and q: read the PACF (for p) and the ACF (for q) of the differenced series.
  3. Compare candidate models with AIC or BIC (lower is better), or use an automated search such as auto_arima from the pmdarima library.
  4. Check the residuals: they should look like white noise (for example with a Ljung-Box test).
Q18What are SARIMA and SARIMAX?
  • SARIMA adds a seasonal part (P, D, Q, s) to ARIMA, where s is the season length (12 for monthly data, 7 for daily data with a weekly pattern).
  • SARIMAX also accepts exogenous variables (external regressors such as price or holidays).
from statsmodels.tsa.statespace.sarimax import SARIMAX

model = SARIMAX(train, order=(1, 1, 1), seasonal_order=(1, 1, 1, 12)).fit()
forecast = model.forecast(steps=12)

For forecasting with exogenous variables, you need to know (or forecast) their future values too.

Q19What are the limitations of ARIMA-type models?
  • They assume a linear relationship and (after differencing) stationarity.
  • They model one series at a time (univariate), so they do not share information across many series.
  • Only one seasonal pattern is handled by SARIMA, so multiple seasonalities are awkward.
  • They need careful tuning, and can be slow on thousands of series.
  • They are weaker for long horizons, sudden structural changes and very noisy data.
Q20What is Prophet, and when is it useful?

Prophet (from Meta) is a forecasting library that fits an additive model: a piecewise trend (with automatic changepoints), seasonalities (yearly, weekly, daily) and holiday effects. It is useful for business series with strong seasonality, holidays and missing data or outliers, and it is easy to use and explain. It is less suitable when the series is driven mainly by short-term autocorrelation, and it does not always beat well-tuned alternatives, so compare it with baselines.

Q21What is a VAR model, and what is Granger causality?

VAR (Vector Autoregression) models several time series together: each variable is predicted from the past values of itself and of all the other variables. It suits related series such as sales and advertising spend.

Granger causality tests whether the past values of series X improve the forecast of series Y. It shows predictive usefulness, not true cause and effect.

Q22What are ARCH and GARCH models used for?

They model time-varying volatility, meaning that the variance of the errors changes over time, with calm periods and turbulent periods. This is typical in financial returns. GARCH forecasts the variance (risk), not the level of the series, and is used in risk management and option pricing.

Machine learning and deep learning for time series

Q23How do you use machine learning models such as XGBoost or LightGBM for forecasting?

Turn the series into a supervised learning table: the target is the value at time t, and the features are lags, rolling statistics, calendar features and external variables. Then train the model on the past and validate on a later period.

Notes:

  • Tree-based models cannot extrapolate a trend beyond the values seen in training, so detrend or difference the target, or model the trend separately.
  • Use time-based validation, never random splits.
  • Multi-step forecasts need a strategy (recursive, direct or multi-output).
  • They work well with many related series and many external features.
Q24When would you use LSTM, GRU or transformer models for time series?
  • LSTM and GRU are recurrent networks that learn patterns across sequences, and can model non-linear and multivariate relationships.
  • Transformer-based models (for example Temporal Fusion Transformer, PatchTST) handle long-range dependencies and many inputs, and N-BEATS is a strong pure deep learning forecaster.

Deep learning tends to pay off when you have a lot of data, many related series, complex patterns or many external features. On a single short series, classical methods are usually as good, faster and easier to explain.

Q25Classical vs machine learning vs deep learning for forecasting: how do you choose?
Classical (ARIMA, ETS) Machine learning (GBMs) Deep learning
Data needed Small Medium Large
Many series / many features Weak Strong Strong
Interpretability High Medium Low
Training effort Low Medium High
Handles trend extrapolation Yes Needs care Needs care

Start with baselines and a classical model, then try a gradient-boosting model with good features, and move to deep learning only if the data and the gain justify the complexity.

Q26What are pretrained time series foundation models?

They are large models trained on many different time series, which can produce zero-shot forecasts on new series without training, or be fine-tuned. Examples include Chronos and TimesFM. They are a promising shortcut when you have little history, but you should compare them against simple baselines on your own data before relying on them.

Evaluation and validation

Q27How do you split data for training and testing a time series model?

Always split chronologically: train on the earlier period and test on the later period (for example the last 3 months or the last full season), so the test imitates real forecasting. Never use a random split or shuffle, because it leaks future information into training.

Q28What is walk-forward (rolling-origin) cross-validation?

Instead of a single split, you repeatedly train on the past and test on the next window, moving the cutoff forward each time. This gives several performance estimates that respect time order. You can use an expanding window (train on all history so far) or a sliding window (fixed-length training period).

from sklearn.model_selection import TimeSeriesSplit

tscv = TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=5)
for train_idx, test_idx in tscv.split(X):
    X_train, X_test = X.iloc[train_idx], X.iloc[test_idx]
    y_train, y_test = y.iloc[train_idx], y.iloc[test_idx]
    # fit on the train part, evaluate on the test part
Q29What metrics are used to evaluate forecasts?
Metric Meaning Note
MAE Average absolute error Easy to explain, same units as the data
RMSE Square root of the average squared error Penalizes large errors more
MAPE Average absolute error as a percentage of the actual value Breaks down when actual values are zero or near zero
sMAPE A symmetric version of the percentage error Handles small values better than MAPE, but has its own quirks
MASE MAE divided by the MAE of a naive forecast Scale-free, below 1 means better than the naive baseline

Choose the metric that matches the business cost of errors, and report it against a baseline.

Q30How do you check the residuals of a forecasting model?

Good residuals should look like white noise: mean near zero, constant variance, no autocorrelation and roughly normal. Check by plotting them, looking at their ACF, and running a Ljung-Box test (a p-value above 0.05 means no significant autocorrelation is left).

from statsmodels.stats.diagnostic import acorr_ljungbox

acorr_ljungbox(model.resid, lags=[10], return_df=True)

Remaining patterns in the residuals mean the model has missed some structure.

Q31What are the strategies for multi-step forecasting?
  • Recursive: one model predicts one step ahead, and its predictions are fed back as inputs for the next step. Simple, but errors accumulate.
  • Direct: train a separate model for each horizon (t+1, t+2, ...). More accurate per step, but needs many models.
  • Multi-output: one model predicts the whole horizon at once (common with neural networks).
Q32How do you express uncertainty in a forecast?

Provide prediction intervals (for example an 80% or 95% range), not only a single number. Classical models give intervals from their error assumptions, and for machine learning models you can use quantile regression, bootstrapping or conformal prediction. Intervals help decision makers plan for the best and worst cases, for example how much stock to hold.

Q33What is data leakage in time series, and how do you avoid it?

Leakage means the model sees information from the future during training. Typical causes:

  • random train/test splits or shuffled cross-validation,
  • rolling features that include the current or future values (use shift first),
  • fitting scalers or imputers on the whole dataset instead of on the training period only,
  • features that would not be known at forecast time (for example the actual weather, when only a forecast would be available).

Always simulate the real forecasting situation: what do you know at the moment of prediction?

Practice and scenarios

Q34How do you detect anomalies in a time series?
  • Statistical: flag points whose z-score or IQR distance is large, usually after removing trend and seasonality.
  • Decomposition-based: run STL and look for large residuals.
  • Forecast-based: predict the expected value with an interval, and flag actual values outside the interval.
  • Machine learning: Isolation Forest, one-class models, or autoencoders (for example LSTM autoencoders) on windows of data.

Decide whether you need real-time detection, and how to handle false alarms and level shifts.

Q35How do you handle multiple seasonalities (for example daily, weekly and yearly patterns)?

SARIMA handles only one seasonal period, so use methods designed for several:

  • Prophet, which supports multiple seasonalities.
  • TBATS and similar models.
  • Fourier terms as regressors for the seasonal patterns.
  • Machine learning with calendar features (hour, day of week, month, holiday flags).
Q36How do you handle holidays, promotions and special events?

Add them as external regressors (binary flags, days before and after an event) in SARIMAX, Prophet or a machine learning model. If they are one-off events, mark them as outliers or model them separately so they do not distort the normal pattern. For future events, you must know the dates in advance.

Q37How do you forecast intermittent demand (many zeros), such as spare parts?

Standard methods and percentage metrics work poorly when many periods have zero demand. Use methods built for intermittent series such as Croston’s method (and its variants SBA and TSB), which model the size of the demand and the interval between demands separately. Evaluate with scale-free metrics such as MASE rather than MAPE.

Q38How do you forecast a new product with no history (cold start)?

Borrow information from similar products: use analogous products and their launch curves, hierarchical or grouped models trained on related series, product attributes (category, price) as features, and judgmental inputs from the business. Update the forecast quickly as the first real sales arrive.

Q39How do you keep a forecasting model accurate over time?
  • Monitor forecast error against actuals continuously, and compare it with the baseline.
  • Retrain regularly on new data, or when the error rises above a threshold.
  • Watch for structural changes (a new competitor, a policy or price change, a pandemic) that make old patterns invalid.
  • Keep a champion-challenger setup: test new models against the current one before replacing it.
Q40Design question: how would you build a demand forecasting system for a retail chain?
  1. Define the need: what to forecast (SKU by store by day), the horizon, how the forecast will be used (replenishment) and the cost of over- and under-stocking.
  2. Data: sales history, prices, promotions, holidays, weather, stock-outs (censored demand), and product and store attributes. Clean and aggregate it at the right level.
  3. Baselines: seasonal naive and ETS.
  4. Models: a gradient-boosting model with lag, rolling and calendar features across all series, and possibly a hierarchical approach so store, region and total forecasts agree. Use a deep model only if the data justifies it.
  5. Validation: walk-forward cross-validation, MASE and weighted error metrics, and checks by product group.
  6. Uncertainty: prediction intervals or quantile forecasts to set safety stock.
  7. Cold start and intermittent items: analog products and Croston-type methods.
  8. Deployment and monitoring: a scheduled pipeline, forecast accuracy tracking, drift alerts and regular retraining, with planners able to override forecasts.
