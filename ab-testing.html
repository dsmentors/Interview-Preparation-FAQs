A/B testing fundamentals

Q1What is A/B testing, and when do you use it?

A/B testing is a controlled experiment in which users are randomly split into two (or more) groups: the control (A) sees the current version and the treatment (B) sees a change. You then compare a chosen metric between the groups to decide whether the change causes an improvement.

Use it to decide whether to launch changes such as a new button, a page layout, a pricing page, an email subject line, a recommendation algorithm or an onboarding flow. Because of random assignment, differences in the metric can be attributed to the change rather than to other factors.

Q2What are the key terms in an A/B test?
Term Meaning
Control The existing version (the baseline)
Treatment / variant The new version being tested
Hypothesis A testable statement of what you expect to happen and why
Primary metric The main measure of success (for example conversion rate)
Guardrail metrics Metrics that must not get worse (for example page load time, refunds)
Randomization unit What is randomly assigned: user, session, device, account or store
Significance level (α) The false positive rate you accept, usually 5%
Power (1 − β) The chance of detecting a real effect, usually 80%
MDE The Minimum Detectable Effect: the smallest change you care to detect
Q3What are the steps in running an A/B test?
  1. Define the goal and the hypothesis (for example “a shorter checkout form will increase completed purchases”).
  2. Choose metrics: one primary, a few secondary and guardrail metrics.
  3. Decide the randomization unit and the population to include.
  4. Calculate the sample size and the duration (using baseline, MDE, α and power).
  5. Run the A/A checks and the setup validation (tracking works, groups are balanced).
  6. Launch and monitor for technical problems, without making decisions early.
  7. Analyze after the planned sample is reached: check validity, then the statistics.
  8. Decide and document: launch, iterate or stop, and record what you learned.
Q4How do you write the null and alternative hypotheses for an A/B test?
  • Null hypothesis (H0): there is no difference between control and treatment (for example conversion rates are equal).
  • Alternative hypothesis (H1): there is a difference (two-sided), or the treatment is better (one-sided).
H0: conversion_B = conversion_A
H1: conversion_B != conversion_A

You never “prove” H1. You decide whether the data is surprising enough under H0 (small p-value) to reject it.

Q5What are primary, secondary and guardrail metrics, and how do you choose them?
  • Primary metric: the one metric that decides the test. It should reflect the goal of the change, be sensitive enough to move, and be measurable within the test period (for example checkout conversion rate).
  • Secondary metrics: help explain why the primary moved (add-to-cart rate, average order value).
  • Guardrail metrics: protect against harm (page load time, error rate, unsubscribe rate, refunds, customer support contacts).

Pick them before the test starts, to avoid searching for a metric that “wins” afterwards.

Q6What is the randomization unit, and why does it matter?

It is the entity that is randomly assigned to a group: user, session, device, account, store or region. Choose it to match how the change is experienced:

  • Use the user for most product changes, so one person always sees the same version.
  • Avoid assigning by session when users return often (a person could see both versions).
  • Use a cluster (for example a city or store) when users influence each other.

Analyze at the same level you randomized. If you randomize by user but analyze by page view, the observations are not independent, and the p-values will be too optimistic.

Q7What is the difference between an A/B test, an A/A test and a multivariate test?
Test What it does Used for
A/B test Compares one control with one (or a few) treatment versions Deciding whether a change works
A/A test Compares identical versions Validating the experiment setup: the false positive rate should be about α, and the groups should be balanced
Multivariate test (MVT) Tests combinations of several elements at once (headline × image × button) Finding the best combination, and interactions, but needs much more traffic
Q8When should you not run an A/B test?
  • Not enough traffic or events to reach the required sample size in a reasonable time.
  • The effect takes too long to show (for example long-term retention for annual contracts).
  • Ethical or legal reasons (harming users, price discrimination, health matters).
  • The change is obviously required (bug fixes, legal compliance).
  • Strong network effects that make a simple split invalid.
  • No clear metric or hypothesis.

In these cases consider qualitative research, quasi-experiments or observational analysis.

Statistical foundations

Q9What is a p-value?

The p-value is the probability of seeing a result at least as extreme as the one observed, if the null hypothesis were true (there is really no difference).

A small p-value (below α, usually 0.05) means the data would be unlikely without a real effect, so you reject H0. It is not the probability that H0 is true, and not the probability that the result is a fluke. It also says nothing about the size or the importance of the effect.

Q10What are the significance level (alpha), Type I error, Type II error and power?
Meaning Typical value
α (significance level) Probability of a Type I error: declaring an effect when there is none (false positive) 5%
β Probability of a Type II error: missing a real effect (false negative) 20%
Power (1 − β) Probability of detecting a real effect of the planned size 80%

You cannot reduce both errors without increasing the sample size, so the sample size is how you balance them.

Q11What is the Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE), and how does it affect the sample size?

The MDE is the smallest effect that you want your test to reliably detect, based on what would matter to the business (for example +1 percentage point in conversion). The smaller the MDE, the larger the sample size needed. Cutting the MDE in half roughly quadruples the required sample.

Choose it from business value and cost, not from what you hope to see.

Q12What is a confidence interval, and how do you interpret it in an A/B test?

A 95% confidence interval for the difference between B and A gives a range of plausible values for the true effect. If you repeated the experiment many times, about 95% of intervals built this way would contain the true difference.

Example: a lift of +1.0 percentage points with a 95% interval of +0.15 to +1.85 means the true lift is plausibly between those values. If the interval excludes zero, the result is statistically significant at 5%. It also shows the uncertainty in the size of the effect, which a p-value does not.

Q13Which statistical test should you use for which type of metric?
Metric type Example Typical test
Proportion / conversion Conversion rate, click rate Two-proportion z-test or chi-square test
Mean of a continuous metric Revenue per user, time on site Welch’s t-test
Skewed or non-normal metric Revenue (heavy tail), session length Mann-Whitney U test, bootstrap, or a transformation
Ratio metrics Click-through rate computed as clicks per impression Delta method or bootstrap
Time to event Time to churn Survival analysis
Q14What is the difference between statistical significance and practical significance?
  • Statistical significance: the observed difference is unlikely to be random noise.
  • Practical significance: the difference is large enough to matter to the business, considering cost, risk and effort.

With a huge sample, even a tiny, useless difference can be statistically significant. Always look at the effect size and the confidence interval, and compare it with a predefined practical threshold.

Q15What is the multiple comparisons problem, and how do you deal with it?

If you test many metrics, segments or variants, the chance of at least one false positive grows quickly: with 20 independent tests at α = 5%, you expect about one false “win” by chance.

Ways to handle it:

  • Decide one primary metric in advance.
  • Bonferroni correction: use α / m for m tests (simple but conservative).
  • Benjamini-Hochberg: controls the false discovery rate, less strict.
  • Treat unplanned segment findings as hypotheses to test again, not as results.
Q16How do you calculate the sample size for an A/B test?

You need the baseline rate, the MDE, α and power. For two proportions, the sample size per group is approximately:

n = (z_alpha/2 + z_beta)^2 x [p1(1 - p1) + p2(1 - p2)] / (p2 - p1)^2

Example: baseline conversion 10%, you want to detect an increase to 11%, α = 5% (two-sided) and power = 80%. This gives about 14,700 users per group (about 29,500 in total).

from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportion_effectsize
from statsmodels.stats.power import NormalIndPower

effect = proportion_effectsize(0.11, 0.10)
n = NormalIndPower().solve_power(effect_size=effect, alpha=0.05, power=0.8,
                                 ratio=1, alternative="two-sided")
print(round(n))        # about 14,700 per group

Design and execution

Q17How long should an A/B test run?

Run it until it reaches the planned sample size, and for at least one or two full weekly cycles, so that weekday and weekend behavior are both included. Estimate the duration as:

days = required total sample size / eligible users per day

Do not stop early just because the result looks significant, and do not extend a test indefinitely hoping for significance. Avoid unusual periods (holidays, big campaigns) unless they are part of the question.

Q18What is the peeking problem, and how do you avoid it?

Peeking means checking the p-value repeatedly during the test and stopping as soon as it looks significant. A fixed-sample test assumes one look at the end, so repeated looks greatly increase the false positive rate (even to 20% or more).

Solutions:

  • Decide the sample size and the end date in advance and analyze only then.
  • Use sequential testing methods (for example alpha-spending or SPRT), designed for continuous monitoring.
  • Use a Bayesian approach with a clear decision rule, and stay disciplined about it.
Q19What is Sample Ratio Mismatch (SRM), and how do you detect it?

SRM happens when the actual split of users differs from the planned split (for example 50/50 planned, but 50.9k vs 49.1k observed). It is a sign of a bug in assignment, logging, redirects or filtering, which makes the results untrustworthy.

Check it with a chi-square goodness-of-fit test:

from scipy.stats import chisquare

observed = [50900, 49100]
expected = [50000, 50000]
print(chisquare(observed, f_exp=expected).pvalue)   # a very small p-value (for example < 0.001) signals SRM

If SRM is present, find and fix the cause before interpreting any metric.

Q20What are the novelty effect and the primacy effect?
  • Novelty effect: users react to something new, so the treatment looks better at first, then the effect fades.
  • Primacy effect: users are used to the old experience and need time to adapt, so the treatment looks worse at first, then improves.

Run the test long enough for behavior to settle, look at the effect over time (day by day, new vs returning users), and consider a long-term holdout for important changes.

Q21What are network effects or interference, and how do you handle them?

A standard A/B test assumes that one user’s assignment does not affect other users (no interference). This fails in social networks, marketplaces, ride-sharing and messaging, where users interact: if treated drivers take rides from control drivers, the groups influence each other and the measured effect is biased.

Alternatives:

  • Cluster randomization: assign whole groups (friend clusters, cities, stores).
  • Geo experiments: test in some regions and compare them with others.
  • Switchback tests: switch the whole system between A and B over alternating time periods.
Q22What is CUPED, and why is it used?

CUPED (Controlled-experiment Using Pre-Experiment Data) is a variance reduction technique. It adjusts each user’s metric using a related pre-experiment value (for example last month’s spend):

Y_adjusted = Y - theta x (X - mean(X)),   theta = cov(X, Y) / var(X)

Because the pre-experiment value explains part of the user-to-user variation, the adjusted metric is less noisy. You get smaller confidence intervals, or the same power with a smaller sample, without bias, because X is measured before the treatment.

Q23How should traffic be allocated, and how do you roll out safely?

A 50/50 split gives the most statistical power for two groups. For risky changes, ramp up gradually (for example 1% → 5% → 20% → 50%), monitoring technical and guardrail metrics at each step before collecting the final test data. Use feature flags to switch the change on and off instantly, and keep a small holdout group to measure long-term effects after the launch.

Q24How do you analyze segments and heterogeneous effects?

The overall effect can hide big differences between groups (new vs returning users, mobile vs desktop, countries). Plan a small number of segments in advance and check them, but remember that each additional segment raises the risk of false positives (multiple comparisons) and has a smaller sample. Treat unexpected segment results as ideas for follow-up tests, and check for Simpson’s paradox when group sizes differ between A and B.

Analysis and interpretation

Q25How do you analyze the result of a conversion-rate A/B test?

Suppose A converted 1,000 of 10,000 users (10%) and B converted 1,100 of 10,000 (11%):

from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportions_ztest

z, p = proportions_ztest(count=[1100, 1000], nobs=[10000, 10000])
print(z, p)            # z about 2.31, p about 0.021
  • Absolute lift: +1.0 percentage points. Relative lift: +10%.
  • p-value 0.021 < 0.05: statistically significant.
  • 95% confidence interval for the difference: about +0.15 to +1.85 percentage points.

Before concluding, check that the test was valid (no SRM, planned sample reached), that the guardrail metrics are healthy, and that the lift is large enough to matter.

Q26What does it mean if the test result is not significant?

It means you could not rule out “no difference”, which is not the same as proving there is no effect. Check the confidence interval:

  • A narrow interval around zero suggests that any effect is small, so the change probably does not matter.
  • A wide interval suggests the test was underpowered (too small a sample or too little time), and the result is inconclusive.

Then decide: ship if the change has other benefits (simpler, cheaper), iterate on the idea, or run a larger test.

Q27The primary metric improved but a guardrail metric got worse. What do you do?

Do not just ship. First check that both results are real (valid test, no SRM, significant). Then quantify the trade-off: for example conversion up 2% but page load time up 300 ms, or revenue up while customer complaints rise. Discuss with stakeholders using the business impact on both sides, look for a variant that keeps the benefit without the harm, and document the decision.

Q28A test shows a significant lift after only two days. Should you ship?

No, not on that evidence alone. A fast “win” may come from peeking, a novelty effect, an incomplete weekly cycle, or small samples. Continue until the planned sample size and duration are reached, check the effect over time, and confirm the test is valid. If the result still holds at the planned end, then decide.

Q29What are the most common mistakes in A/B testing?
  • Peeking and stopping early.
  • Underpowered tests (too small a sample or too short a duration).
  • Too many metrics or segments, and cherry-picking the one that “won”.
  • Ignoring SRM and other validity checks.
  • Changing the test midway (new traffic rules, new variants).
  • Wrong unit of analysis (randomize by user, analyze by page view).
  • Ignoring seasonality and weekly cycles.
  • Confusing statistical and practical significance.
  • Not documenting the results and the learning.
Q30How do you measure long-term effects after an A/B test?

Short tests may not show effects such as retention, lifetime value or changing user habits. Options: keep a long-term holdout group that never receives the change, extend key metrics on the original groups for weeks or months, use surrogate (leading) metrics that predict long-term outcomes, and run follow-up experiments.

Advanced topics

Q31What is Bayesian A/B testing, and how does it differ from the frequentist approach?
Frequentist Bayesian
Question answered “If there were no real difference, how surprising is this data?” (p-value) “Given the data, how likely is B better than A?”
Output p-value, confidence interval Posterior probability and a credible interval
Decision rule Reject H0 if p < α For example ship if P(B > A) > 95%, or if the expected loss is small
Stopping Fixed sample size (or sequential corrections) More natural, but still needs a clear rule
Prior knowledge Not used Included as a prior distribution 
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
a = rng.beta(1 + 1000, 1 + 9000, 100_000)     # posterior for A (Beta(1,1) prior)
b = rng.beta(1 + 1100, 1 + 8900, 100_000)     # posterior for B
print("P(B > A) =", (b > a).mean())            # about 0.99

Bayesian results are easier to explain to business teams, but they depend on the chosen prior.

Q32What is a multi-armed bandit, and how is it different from an A/B test?

A bandit algorithm adjusts the traffic split as data arrives, sending more users to the variants that perform better, which balances exploration (learning) and exploitation (earning). A common method is Thompson sampling.

A/B test Multi-armed bandit
Traffic split Fixed Adaptive
Goal Learn the effect precisely and reliably Maximize reward during the experiment
Best for Product decisions needing clear evidence Short-lived options (headlines, promotions), many variants
Weakness Some users get the worse variant for the whole test Harder to get unbiased effect estimates and confidence intervals
Q33What is sequential testing?

Sequential testing methods (SPRT, group sequential designs, alpha-spending functions, always-valid p-values) allow you to look at the results repeatedly during a test and stop early while keeping the false positive rate under control. They are useful when decisions must be made quickly or when a harmful variant should be stopped early, at the cost of a somewhat larger sample if the test runs to the end.

Q34What can you do if you cannot randomize (quasi-experiments)?

When random assignment is impossible (a policy change, a regional launch, a past event), use methods that estimate causal effects from observational data:

  • Difference-in-differences: compare the change over time in the affected group with the change in a similar unaffected group.
  • Matching or propensity scores: compare similar users who did and did not receive the change.
  • Synthetic control: build a weighted combination of control units that imitates the treated unit before the change.
  • Regression discontinuity and instrumental variables for particular situations.

These rely on stronger assumptions than a randomized test, so the conclusions need more caution.

Q35What are feature flags and an experimentation platform?
  • Feature flags let you turn a feature on or off for specific users without deploying new code, which makes gradual rollouts and quick rollbacks easy.
  • An experimentation platform (built in-house, or tools such as Optimizely, Statsig, Eppo or LaunchDarkly) handles assignment, logging, metric calculation, statistical analysis and reporting consistently for all teams.

A good platform prevents common mistakes by making validity checks (SRM, power, guardrails) automatic.

Scenarios and design questions

Q36Design an A/B test for a new checkout button color or design.
  1. Hypothesis: a more visible button will increase the checkout completion rate.
  2. Primary metric: checkout completion rate (users who complete a purchase / users who reach checkout). Secondary: click-through on the button, average order value. Guardrails: page load time, error rate, refund rate.
  3. Unit: the user (so one person always sees the same version). Population: users who reach the checkout page.
  4. Sample size: baseline rate, MDE (for example +1 point), α = 5%, power = 80%, which gives the required users per group, and a duration of at least 1 to 2 full weeks.
  5. Run checks: an A/A or setup check, and an SRM check.
  6. Analyze at the end: z-test and confidence interval, guardrails, segments planned in advance.
  7. Decide with the business impact in mind and document the result.
Q37How would you test a new recommendation algorithm?

Randomize users into the old and new algorithms. Use a primary metric tied to value (click-through rate, conversion or revenue per user), with guardrails (diversity, latency, complaints). Because it may change long-term behavior, run it long enough, check novelty effects, and keep a holdout. Do offline evaluation first to screen candidates, but rely on the online test for the final decision, since offline metrics often do not match real user behavior.

Q38How would you test a price change?

Pricing tests need care: randomizing prices for the same customers can seem unfair and can cause trust or legal problems. Options are to randomize by region or new customers, or to test with time-based or cohort designs. Track revenue and profit per visitor (not only conversion), plus long-term effects like churn and customer satisfaction, because a higher price often reduces conversion but raises margin.

Q39Conversion in B is 11% versus 10% in A, but with only 500 users per group. What do you conclude?

With 500 users per group, the standard error is large (about 1.9 percentage points), so a 1-point difference is well within random noise (the p-value is about 0.6). The test is underpowered, since detecting a 1-point lift needs about 14,700 users per group. The right conclusion is “inconclusive”, not “B is better” or “no effect”: continue to the required sample size or run a larger test.

Q40An A/B test shows a 2% lift for the overall population, but the lift is negative for new users. What would you do?

First check that the segment finding is real (sample size, planned segment, multiple comparisons, SRM). If it holds, understand why new users react differently (for example the change confuses people who have not seen the old design). Options: launch only for the segments that benefit, improve the design for new users and test again, or monitor the long-term effect. Make the decision using the size of each segment and its business value.

Q41How do you explain A/B test results to a non-technical stakeholder?

Lead with the decision and the business impact, in plain language:

“Version B increased purchases by about 10% (from 10.0% to 11.0%). We are 95% confident the real improvement is between 1.5% and 18.5% in relative terms, so it is very likely positive. Page speed and refunds were not affected. We recommend launching to everyone and keeping a small holdout to confirm the long-term effect.”

Use a simple chart, avoid jargon (explain p-values as “the chance this was just luck”), and state any limits or risks honestly.
