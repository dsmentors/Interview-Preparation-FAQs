SDLC fundamentals

Q1What is the Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC)?

The SDLC is a structured process that describes how software is planned, built, tested, delivered and maintained. It breaks the work into phases, each with defined activities and outputs, so teams can deliver software of good quality, on time and within budget.

Q2What are the phases of the SDLC?
Phase What happens Typical output
1. Planning Define the goal, scope, feasibility, budget, timeline and risks Project plan, feasibility study
2. Requirement analysis Gather and document what the business and users need BRD, SRS, user stories
3. Design Plan the architecture, database, interfaces and screens High-level and low-level design, wireframes
4. Development (implementation) Developers write the code Working software modules
5. Testing Verify that the software meets the requirements and find defects Test cases, test reports, defect logs
6. Deployment Release the software to users Production release, release notes
7. Maintenance Fix issues, adapt and improve the software after release Patches, updates, new versions
Q3Why is the SDLC important?
  • Gives the team a clear, shared process and responsibilities.
  • Helps manage scope, time and cost.
  • Improves quality by building review and testing into every phase.
  • Makes risks visible early, when they are cheapest to fix.
  • Provides documentation and traceability for audits and future maintenance.
  • Improves communication between the business and the technical team.
Q4What is the role of a Business Analyst in each phase of the SDLC?
Phase What the BA does
Planning Helps define the business case, scope and objectives; identifies stakeholders
Requirement analysis Elicits, analyzes, documents and validates requirements (BRD, user stories)
Design Clarifies requirements for architects and designers; reviews wireframes and prototypes with stakeholders
Development Answers questions, clarifies requirements and manages requirement changes
Testing Reviews test cases against requirements, supports and coordinates UAT, and helps triage defects
Deployment Prepares user documentation and training, supports the go-live
Maintenance Analyzes change requests, enhancements and the impact of issues
Q5What are the key roles in a software project?
  • Business Analyst: bridges the business and the technical team by defining requirements.
  • Product Owner / Product Manager: owns the product vision and the backlog priorities.
  • Project Manager (or Scrum Master in Scrum): plans, tracks and removes obstacles.
  • Solution / Software Architect: designs the technical structure.
  • Developers: build the software.
  • QA / Test engineers: test and ensure quality.
  • UX/UI designer: designs the user experience and interface.
  • DevOps engineer: automates builds, deployment and infrastructure.
  • Stakeholders and end users: the people who sponsor, use or are affected by the product.
Q6What are the main deliverables (documents) created during the SDLC?
  • BRD (Business Requirements Document) and SRS/FRS (software or functional requirements specification).
  • Requirement Traceability Matrix (RTM).
  • Design documents (HLD, LLD), wireframes and prototypes.
  • Test plan, test cases and test reports.
  • UAT sign-off.
  • Release notes, user manuals and training material.
Q7What is the difference between SDLC and STLC?

SDLC models

Q8What is the Waterfall model, and what are its pros and cons?

Waterfall is a sequential model: each phase (requirements, design, development, testing, deployment, maintenance) is completed before the next begins, with little going back.

Pros Cons
Simple and easy to manage Inflexible: changes are costly and hard
Clear phases, milestones and documentation Working software appears late
Works well when requirements are stable and well understood Testing and user feedback come at the end, so problems are found late
Q9What is the V-model?

The V-model is an extension of Waterfall where every development phase is paired with a testing phase:

Development phase Matching test level
Business requirements Acceptance testing (UAT)
System requirements System testing
High-level design Integration testing
Low-level design Unit testing

Test planning starts early, together with the corresponding development phase, which improves quality. It still shares Waterfall’s rigidity.

Q10What are the iterative and incremental models?
  • Iterative: build a rough version of the whole product, then improve it repeatedly based on feedback.
  • Incremental: build the product in pieces (increments), each delivering a working part of the functionality.

Most modern approaches, including Agile, combine both: small working increments delivered in repeated cycles. They give early feedback and handle change better than Waterfall.

Q11What is the Spiral model?

The Spiral model is risk-driven. The project goes through repeated loops (spirals), and each loop has four steps: planning, risk analysis, engineering (development and testing), and evaluation by the customer. It suits large, complex and high-risk projects, but it is expensive and needs strong risk-analysis skills.

Q12What is the Prototype model?

A prototype (a quick, simplified working model or mock-up) is built early and shown to users to collect feedback. It is refined until the requirements are clear, and then the real system is built. It is useful when requirements are unclear or the user interface is critical. The risk is that users mistake the prototype for the finished product, or that the throwaway prototype gets stretched into production.

Q13What is the Agile model?

Agile is an iterative and incremental approach that delivers working software in short cycles (often 1 to 4 weeks), values collaboration with customers and welcomes changing requirements. Planning, development and testing happen together in each cycle. Common Agile frameworks are Scrum, Kanban, XP (Extreme Programming) and SAFe for large organizations.

Q14Compare the common SDLC models. How do you choose one?
Model Flexibility to change Customer involvement Best for
Waterfall Low Low (at start and end) Stable, well-defined requirements, regulated projects
V-model Low Low Projects where testing rigor is critical (for example medical, safety)
Iterative / Incremental Medium Medium Large projects delivered in parts
Spiral Medium Medium Large, high-risk, complex projects
Agile (Scrum, Kanban) High High, continuous Changing requirements, products that need fast feedback

Choose by looking at how stable the requirements are, the risk, project size, regulatory needs, customer availability, team experience and the need for speed.

Q15What is a hybrid approach (Water-Scrum-Fall)?

Many organizations mix approaches: for example, upfront planning, budgeting and requirement scoping done in a Waterfall way, development done in Agile sprints, and a formal release process at the end. It fits companies with fixed budgets, compliance requirements or traditional governance that still want the benefits of iterative development.

Agile and Scrum

Q16What are the four values and the principles of the Agile Manifesto?

The Manifesto values:

  1. Individuals and interactions over processes and tools.
  2. Working software over comprehensive documentation.
  3. Customer collaboration over contract negotiation.
  4. Responding to change over following a plan.

The items on the right still matter, but the items on the left are valued more. The manifesto also has 12 principles, such as satisfying the customer through early and continuous delivery, welcoming changing requirements, delivering working software frequently, sustainable pace and regular reflection to improve.

Q17What is Scrum? Explain its roles, events and artifacts.

Scrum is a lightweight Agile framework in which a small team delivers work in fixed-length iterations called sprints (one month or less).

Category Items
Roles Product Owner (maximizes product value, owns the backlog), Scrum Master (helps the team follow Scrum and removes obstacles), Developers (build the increment)
Events Sprint, Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum (15 minutes), Sprint Review, Sprint Retrospective
Artifacts Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog, Increment
Q18Explain the Scrum events.
  • Sprint: the time-boxed iteration (one month or less) in which a usable increment is built.
  • Sprint Planning: the team decides what to deliver in the sprint and how, and defines the sprint goal.
  • Daily Scrum: a 15-minute meeting for developers to inspect progress toward the sprint goal and adjust the plan.
  • Sprint Review: the team shows the increment to stakeholders and collects feedback.
  • Sprint Retrospective: the team reflects on how it worked and decides on improvements for the next sprint.
Q19What is the difference between the Product Backlog and the Sprint Backlog?
Product Backlog Sprint Backlog
What it is The ordered list of everything that might be needed in the product The items selected for the current sprint, plus the plan to deliver them
Owner Product Owner Developers
Changes Continuously refined and reprioritized Changes only within the sprint, by the team
Lifetime Lives as long as the product Lasts for one sprint
Q20What is a user story, and what makes a good one (INVEST)?

A user story is a short description of a feature from the user’s point of view:

As a [type of user], I want [goal], so that [benefit].

A good story follows INVEST: Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small and Testable.

Q21What are acceptance criteria, and how are they written?

Acceptance criteria are the conditions that a story must meet to be accepted by the Product Owner. They make the story testable and avoid misunderstandings. A popular format is Given / When / Then:

Given a registered user is on the login page
When they enter a valid email and password
Then they are taken to the dashboard
Q22What is the difference between the Definition of Ready and the Definition of Done?
  • Definition of Ready (DoR): the checklist a backlog item must meet before it can enter a sprint (clear description, acceptance criteria, estimated, dependencies known).
  • Definition of Done (DoD): the shared checklist that an item must meet to be considered complete (coded, reviewed, tested, documented, accepted).

The DoD is a part of Scrum itself, while the DoR is a common practice that teams add.

Q23What are story points, and how does the team estimate?

Story points are a relative measure of size (effort, complexity and uncertainty) of a backlog item, not hours. Teams compare an item with others they know. A common technique is planning poker: each member picks a number from a scale such as the Fibonacci sequence (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13) and the team discusses differences until they agree.

Velocity is the number of points a team completes per sprint, and it is used to forecast how much work the team can do in the future.

Q24What is the difference between Scrum and Kanban?
Scrum Kanban
Cadence Fixed-length sprints Continuous flow, no fixed iterations
Roles Product Owner, Scrum Master, Developers No required roles
Work limits Sprint capacity WIP (work-in-progress) limits per column
Changes during the cycle Avoided inside a sprint Can be added any time if the WIP limit allows
Metrics Velocity, burndown Cycle time, lead time, throughput
Good for Product development with planned increments Support, maintenance and continuous work
Q25What is backlog refinement (grooming)?

It is the ongoing activity of reviewing, clarifying, splitting, estimating and ordering backlog items so that the top of the backlog is ready for upcoming sprints. The Product Owner and the team work on it together, with the BA often helping to detail the requirements and acceptance criteria.

Q26What is a burndown chart and what does velocity tell you?
  • A burndown chart shows the remaining work (in story points or hours) against time during a sprint or release. A line that falls steadily towards zero means the team is on track.
  • Velocity shows how much the team completes per sprint, which helps with planning and spotting changes in the team’s capacity.

Use both as tools for conversation and improvement, not to judge or compare teams.

Q27What is the role of a Business Analyst in a Scrum team?

Scrum has no official “BA” role, but the work still needs to be done. In practice a BA often supports the Product Owner by analyzing needs, writing and refining user stories with acceptance criteria, prioritizing with stakeholders, preparing for sprint planning, clarifying questions during the sprint, supporting testing and UAT, and communicating with stakeholders.

Requirements, design and documentation

Q28What is the difference between BRD, FRS and SRS?
Document Focus Written by / for
BRD (Business Requirements Document) Why the project exists: business goals, scope, high-level business needs BA, for business stakeholders
FRS (Functional Requirements Specification) What the system should do, in functional terms BA, for the project team
SRS (Software Requirements Specification) Detailed functional and non-functional requirements, system behavior and constraints BA or system analysts, for developers and testers

The exact naming varies between companies.

Q29What is a Requirement Traceability Matrix (RTM)?

An RTM is a table that links each requirement to its design, development and test items. It shows that every requirement is implemented and tested, helps assess the impact of a change, and prevents scope gaps and unneeded features.

Req ID Requirement Design item Test case Status
R-101 User can reset password DES-12 TC-45 Passed
Q30What is the difference between a use case and a user story?
Use case User story
Detail More detailed: actors, preconditions, main flow, alternative flows A short sentence plus acceptance criteria
Style Formal, often used in traditional projects Light, used in Agile projects
Purpose Describes the full interaction with the system A conversation starter, to be refined with the team
Q31What are wireframes and prototypes, and why use them?
  • A wireframe is a simple, low-detail layout of a screen that shows structure and content.
  • A prototype is an interactive mock-up that simulates how the product will work.

They let stakeholders see and react before development starts, which reveals missing or wrong requirements early, when changes are cheap. Tools include Figma, Balsamiq and Axure.

Q32What is the difference between high-level design (HLD) and low-level design (LLD)?
  • HLD: the overall architecture: main components, how they interact, technology choices, data flow and databases.
  • LLD: the detailed design of each component: classes, methods, database tables and columns, algorithms and interfaces.

Architects usually create the HLD, and developers or designers create the LLD from it.

Testing, deployment and maintenance

Q33What are the levels of software testing?
Level What it checks Done by
Unit testing Individual functions or components Developers
Integration testing That modules work together Developers and testers
System testing The whole system against the requirements QA team
User Acceptance Testing (UAT) That the system meets the business needs and is ready for use Business users and the client, supported by the BA
Q34What is UAT, and what is the BA’s role in it?

User Acceptance Testing is the final validation, done by business users in a production-like environment, to confirm that the software meets the business requirements and can go live.

The BA typically prepares UAT scenarios and test cases from the requirements, coordinates the users and the schedule, supports the testers, helps log and triage defects, and gets the formal sign-off.

Q35What is the difference between verification and validation?
  • Verification: “Are we building the product right?” It checks that the software meets its specification, through reviews, inspections and testing.
  • Validation: “Are we building the right product?” It checks that the software meets the user’s real needs, for example through UAT.
Q36What is regression testing?

Regression testing re-runs existing tests after a change (a new feature, a bug fix or a configuration change) to make sure that previously working functionality has not broken. It is usually automated, because it has to be repeated often.

Q37What is the defect life cycle, and how do severity and priority differ?

A typical defect life cycle: New → Assigned → Open (in progress) → Fixed → Retest → Verified → Closed, with Reopened if the fix fails, and states such as Rejected or Deferred.

  • Severity: how badly the defect affects the system (a crash is high severity). Usually set by testers.
  • Priority: how urgently it should be fixed. Set by the business or product owner.

A typo on the company logo can be low severity but high priority.

Q38What are the types of software maintenance?
Type Purpose Example
Corrective Fix defects found after release Fix a calculation bug
Adaptive Adjust to a changed environment Support a new operating system or regulation
Perfective Improve performance or add features Speed up a slow report
Preventive Prevent future problems Refactor code to reduce risk
Q39What are the typical environments in a software project?

Code moves through separate environments: Development (developers build and test), Test or QA (testers verify), Staging or UAT (a production-like copy for final checks and acceptance) and Production (live users). Keeping them separate protects real users from unfinished or faulty changes.

Q40What are DevOps and CI/CD, and how do they relate to the SDLC?

DevOps brings development and operations together to deliver software faster and more reliably, with automation and shared responsibility.

  • Continuous Integration (CI): developers merge changes often, and each change is automatically built and tested.
  • Continuous Delivery/Deployment (CD): tested changes are automatically prepared for release (delivery) or released to production (deployment).

They shorten the SDLC feedback loop and make releases frequent, small and low-risk.

Management, risk and scenarios

Q41What is scope creep, and how do you prevent it?

Scope creep is the uncontrolled growth of project scope after it has been agreed, without matching changes in time, budget or resources. Prevent it with:

  • a clearly documented and signed-off scope,
  • a formal change control process (assess impact, approve, document),
  • clear prioritization of requests (for example MoSCoW),
  • regular communication with stakeholders, and
  • in Agile, adding new items to the backlog and trading them against existing work.
Q42How do you handle changing requirements in Waterfall vs Agile?
  • Waterfall: changes go through a formal change request: impact analysis on cost, time and scope, approval by a change board, then updates to all documents and plans.
  • Agile: change is expected. New or changed items are added to the product backlog, prioritized by the Product Owner, and picked up in a later sprint, with the current sprint protected.
Q43How do you identify and manage risks in a project?
  1. Identify risks (technical, schedule, budget, people, external) with the team and stakeholders.
  2. Assess each one for probability and impact.
  3. Plan a response: avoid, reduce (mitigate), transfer or accept it.
  4. Record them in a risk register with an owner.
  5. Monitor and review regularly.
Q44Why do software projects fail, and how can a BA help prevent it?

Common causes: unclear or changing requirements, poor communication, unrealistic estimates or deadlines, weak stakeholder involvement, scope creep, poor testing, and no clear ownership.

A BA helps by clarifying and validating requirements early, keeping documentation and traceability, involving stakeholders continuously, managing changes, spotting risks and gaps, and making sure the delivered solution solves the real business problem.

Q45How do you measure the success of a software project?
  • Delivery: delivered on time, within budget and scope.
  • Quality: number and severity of defects, and test coverage.
  • Business value: the business goals and KPIs achieved (cost saved, revenue, efficiency).
  • User adoption and satisfaction.
  • Stakeholder satisfaction.
  • In Agile: velocity trends, lead time and customer feedback.
Q46How does the lifecycle differ for a data science or machine learning project?

Data projects are more experimental and data-driven, so a common process is CRISP-DM: business understanding, data understanding, data preparation, modeling, evaluation and deployment, repeated in cycles. Compared with the usual SDLC, they also need data quality work, model monitoring and retraining after deployment, because the model’s performance can change as the data changes (see MLOps).

Q47Scenario: which SDLC model would you choose for a startup building an MVP, and which for a banking compliance system?
  • Startup MVP: Agile (Scrum or Kanban). Requirements are uncertain and speed and feedback matter most, so build small increments, release early and adapt to what users do.
  • Banking compliance system: Waterfall, V-model or a hybrid. Requirements are fixed by regulation, traceability and documentation are mandatory, and the cost of errors is high. Use a hybrid with Agile development inside a controlled, documented process where possible.

The point is to justify the choice from requirement stability, risk, regulation and customer involvement.

Q48Behavioral: tell me about a project where requirements changed late. How did you handle it?

A strong answer follows STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result):

  1. Situation: the project and the late change (for example a new regulation or a stakeholder request).
  2. Task: your responsibility as the BA.
  3. Action: you assessed the impact on scope, time and cost, discussed options with stakeholders, documented and got approval for the change, updated the requirements and the traceability, and communicated it to the team.
  4. Result: delivered with an agreed trade-off, with measurable results and what you learned.

Prepare one or two real examples in advance.
